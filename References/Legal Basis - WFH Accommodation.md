# Legal Basis — Do Ken's Functional Limitations Support a WFH Accommodation?

Prepared: 2026-10-27. Grounded in the OHRC policies held in this folder:
- `OHRC - Policy on preventing discrimination based on mental health disabilities and addictions` (2014) — https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/policy-preventing-discrimination-based-mental-health-disabilities-and-addictions
- `OHRC - Policy on ableism and discrimination based on disability` (2016) — https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/policy-ableism-and-discrimination-based-disability
- `OHRC - Policy and guidelines on disability and the duty to accommodate`

Section references below are to these policies unless noted. This document is
for Ken, his OPSEU rep, and his lawyer. It states the legal position only; it
does not speculate about diagnoses or fabricate facts.

## OHRC FACT — what the law says explicitly

### 1. Addiction is a protected disability; relapse risk is part of it

> "Drug and alcohol addictions are disabilities under the Code."
> — Mental Health & Addictions Policy, s. 4.2

> "Like other chronic diseases, it can be progressive, **relapsing** and fatal."
> — Mental Health & Addictions Policy, s. 4.2 (quoting the medical definition)

Therefore "commuting triggers relapse risk" is not a peripheral concern — the
risk of relapse is inherent to the disability itself. This is the anchor
limitation of the case.

### 2. Flexible work arrangements are expressly recognized accommodations

> Employment: "allowing a flexible work schedule"
> — Mental Health & Addictions Policy, s. 13.5 (Forms of accommodation)

> "making attendance requirements flexible, where possible, if non-attendance
> can be shown to be linked to a disability"
> — Mental Health & Addictions Policy, s. 13.5

### 3. The accommodation only has to meet the person's needs

> "Accommodation is considered appropriate if it … **meets the individual's
> disability-related needs**."
> — Mental Health & Addictions Policy, s. 13.3

### 4. The employer chooses between equally effective options, not between a
   working and a non-working option

> "If there is a choice between two accommodations that equally respond to the
> person's needs in a dignified way, then the accommodation provider is
> entitled to select the one that is less expensive or less disruptive."
> — s. 13.3; Ableism Policy, s. 8.3

## INTERPRETATION — the framework that decides this case

The analysis proceeds in four steps (Meiorin framework; Code ss. 11 and 17):

| Step | Test | Application to Ken |
|------|------|--------------------|
| 1. Prima facie discrimination (s. 11) | A neutral rule adversely affects a Code ground | The 5-day RTO mandate adversely affects Ken's disability (commuting → relapse risk) ✅ |
| 2. Essential duties (s. 17; Ableism s. 8.3.1) | Accommodation relates only to *essential* duties | In-office attendance / commuting is **not** an essential duty of a remote-compatible IT role Ken already performed remotely ✅✅ |
| 3. Appropriate accommodation (s. 8.3/13.3) | Must meet the disability-related needs | WFH removes the commuting trigger — it meets the need ✅ |
| 4. Undue hardship (s. 9) | Employer must show it *cannot* accommodate | High bar (cost, health & safety, outside sources); unlikely for an already-remote role ✅ |

### The single strongest hook

Ken's role is **fully remote-compatible** (worked remotely under an AWA and
through COVID). That makes the RTO mandate a **non-essential requirement** that
adversely affects a protected ground — precisely the situation s. 11 puts on
the employer to justify, and that s. 17 takes off the table. The employer
would have to show WFH is *impossible* without undue hardship — not merely
inconvenient.

### Essential duties (the decisive point)

> "In employment, essential duties are the 'vital' or 'indispensable' aspects
> of someone's job." — Ableism Policy, s. 8.3.1

Commuting to a building is not a "vital or indispensable" aspect of a job whose
duties (software development, team leadership) are performed on a computer and
were performed remotely for years. Under s. 17, an employer cannot judge a
person incapable of the essential duties without accommodating — and here the
essential duties are not in issue at all.

## PRACTICAL GUIDANCE — caveats and how to respond

1. **"Stress" alone is not enough.** The policy warns that *mere assertions of
   "stress" or "anxiety" may not be enough* to establish a disability — they
   must *"make it clear that these symptoms relate to a disability"* (s. 13.8
   of the Mental Health & Addictions Policy). Ken is covered: he has diagnosed
   disabilities (AUD in sustained remission, Social Anxiety Disorder, Specific
   Phobia – fear of driving) and the note ties every limitation to them. **Keep
   that tie explicit in every document.**

2. **The contest is scope, not entitlement.** The law supports accommodation
   *to the point of undue hardship*; Peter's report suggests 2–3 days may meet
   the need. If the employer offers reduced days, the question becomes whether
   *any* commuting triggers the limitation. Ken's journal evidence (the
   WFH-vs-commute contrast) argues the trigger is the commute itself, not its
   frequency. This is the gap to close — already addressed in
   `Psychologist/EditsForPeter.md` §1.

3. **Precedent cuts in Ken's favour.** *Hall v. Chief of Police, Ottawa Police
   Service* (2008 CanLII 65766, cited in the policy) treated "significant risk
   of relapse" as a real, weight-bearing limitation, but found undue hardship
   **only because it was a safety-sensitive police role**. Ken's IT role is not
   safety-sensitive, so the principle (relapse risk is real) applies while the
   limiting factor (safety sensitivity) does not.

4. **No OHRC policy text names "commuting" specifically.** The case rests on
   the essential-duties + adverse-effect + undue-hardship framework above, not
   on a commuting-specific rule. The framework does the work.

---

## Bottom line

Ken's limitations are **legally sufficient grounds** for a WFH accommodation
request. The protection is explicit for addiction (including relapse risk), and
the role's remote-compatibility removes the employer's two main defences
(essential duty and undue hardship). The only genuine contest is **scope**
(full remote vs. reduced days), not **entitlement**.
